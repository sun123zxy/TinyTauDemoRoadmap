Fermat’s Last Theorem Over Function Fields and for Regular Primes

A Unified Arithmetic and Algebro-Geometric Exposition

Author

Gemini 4 Argon

Published

November 2, 2026

Abstract

We present a unified, self-contained exposition of Fermat’s Last Theorem in two classical settings: the polynomial ring \(k[t]\) over a field \(k\) of characteristic zero, and the ring of rational integers \(\mathbb{Z}\) for regular prime exponents \(p\). By framing both problems over a base principal ideal domain \(A\) and passing to its integral closure \(B\) in a cyclotomic extension, we isolate the three structural invariants governing the Diophantine equation \(x^n + y^n = z^n\): the ramification of the cyclotomic uniformizer \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta_n\) in \(B\), the \(n\)-torsion of the Picard group \(\operatorname{Pic}(\operatorname{Spec} B)\), and the structure of the unit quotient \(B^\times / (B^\times)^n\). In the function field case \(A = k[t]\), we complement the cyclotomic factorization and infinite descent proof with its differential and algebro-geometric counterparts via Kähler differentials, the Mason–Stothers theorem, and the Riemann–Hurwitz genus formula applied to the Fermat curve. In the arithmetic case \(A = \mathbb{Z}\), we develop the arithmetic of the cyclotomic ring \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) from first principles and establish Kummer’s theorem for both Case I (\(p \nmid xyz\)) and Case II (\(p \mid xyz\)).

1 A Unified Diophantine Framework over Dedekind Domains

The classical analogy between the polynomial ring \(k[t]\) over a field \(k\) of characteristic zero and the ring of rational integers \(\mathbb{Z}\) rests on a shared commutative-algebraic foundation: both are principal ideal domains of Krull dimension one. From the scheme-theoretic perspective, \(\operatorname{Spec} k[t] = \mathbb{A}^1_k\) and \(\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}\) are regular one-dimensional affine schemes whose closed points correspond to maximal ideals and whose local rings at closed points are discrete valuation rings. When we study the Fermat equation \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) for an integer exponent \(n \ge 3\) over \(A = k[t]\) or \(A = \mathbb{Z}\), the additive relation on the left-hand side obstructs direct divisibility arguments in \(A\). To convert this additive problem into a multiplicative one, we pass to a finite extension of the fraction field \(F = \operatorname{Frac}(A)\) generated by a primitive root of unity and work in the integral closure \(B\) of \(A\).

Remark 1 (Affine Schemes of Dedekind Domains). Recall from basic scheme theory (Hartshorne 1977, ch. II, 6) that an integral domain \(B\) is a Dedekind domain if and only if it is noetherian, integrally closed in its fraction field \(K = \operatorname{Frac}(B)\), and of Krull dimension at most one (every nonzero prime ideal is maximal). Geometrically, the affine scheme \(X = \operatorname{Spec} B\) consists of a unique generic point \(\eta = (0)\) with residue field \(\kappa(\eta) = K\), together with closed points \(x = \mathfrak{q} \in \operatorname{MaxSpec}(B)\) whose local rings \(\mathcal{O}_{X,x} = B_{\mathfrak{q}}\) are discrete valuation rings with uniformizers \(\pi_{\mathfrak{q}}\) and normalized discrete valuations \(v_{\mathfrak{q}} \colon K^\times \twoheadrightarrow \mathbb{Z}\). Every nonzero fractional ideal \(\mathfrak{a} \subset K\) defines an invertible \(\mathcal{O}_X\)-module (a line bundle on \(X\)), and unique factorization of ideals in \(B\) is the statement that the Weil divisor map \(\operatorname{FracId}(B) \to \operatorname{Div}(X)\), \(\mathfrak{a} \mapsto \sum_{\mathfrak{q}} v_{\mathfrak{q}}(\mathfrak{a})[\mathfrak{q}]\), is an isomorphism of abelian groups. The quotient of \(\operatorname{FracId}(B)\) by the subgroup \(\operatorname{PrinId}(B)\) of principal fractional ideals is canonically isomorphic to the Picard group \(\operatorname{Pic}(X) = \operatorname{Cl}(B)\) of isomorphism classes of line bundles on \(X\).

To formulate the problem without ambiguity across both rings, we first specify what constitutes a nontrivial primitive solution over a general principal ideal domain \(A\). In \(\mathbb{Z}\), a solution such as \((1, 0, 1)\) is trivial because one coordinate vanishes; in \(k[t]\), any solution with \(x, y, z \in k\) is already constant and belongs to the ground field rather than the function field geometry of \(\mathbb{A}^1_k\).

Definition 1 (Primitive Fermat Solutions over a PID) Let \(A\) be a principal ideal domain and let \(n \ge 3\) be an integer. A triple \((x, y, z) \in A^3\) is called a primitive solution to the Fermat equation of exponent \(n\) over \(A\) if:

  1. \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) in \(A\);
  2. \(x, y, z\) are pairwise coprime in \(A\), meaning that \((x, y) = (y, z) = (z, x) = A\);
  3. \(x y z \neq 0\), and \((x, y, z)\) is not a triple of units in \(A^\times\).

Note that in any solution to \(x^n + y^n = z^n\), any prime element \(\pi \in A\) dividing two of \(x, y, z\) automatically divides the third; dividing through by \(\gcd(x, y, z)^n\) reduces any nonzero solution to a pairwise coprime one. For \(A = \mathbb{Z}\), condition (3) simply requires \(x y z \neq 0\) (since \((\pm 1)^n + (\pm 1)^n = (\pm 1)^n\) is impossible in \(\mathbb{Z}\)). For \(A = k[t]\), where \(A^\times = k^\times\), condition (3) requires that \(x, y, z\) are not all constant polynomials in \(k\).

Before introducing cyclotomic extensions, we record the standard reduction of the exponent \(n \ge 3\) to odd prime exponents \(p\) (together with \(n = 4\)). Over \(k[t]\), our proofs in the next two sections will handle all exponents \(n \ge 3\) directly and uniformly, whereas over \(\mathbb{Z}\) the arithmetic of prime cyclotomic fields \(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)\) is markedly simpler than that of composite cyclotomic fields.

Proposition 1 (Reduction to Prime Exponents and Four) Let \(A\) be a principal ideal domain. If the Fermat equation \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) admits no primitive solutions over \(A\) for \(n = 4\) and for every odd prime \(n = p\), then it admits no primitive solutions over \(A\) for any integer \(n \ge 3\).

Proof. Every integer \(n \ge 3\) is divisible either by \(4\) or by an odd prime \(p\). Write \(n = m d\), where \(m \in \{4\} \cup \{p \mid p \text{ odd prime}\}\) and \(d \ge 1\) is an integer. If \((x, y, z) \in A^3\) is a primitive solution to \(x^n + y^n = z^n\), then \((x^d, y^d, z^d) \in A^3\) satisfies \((x^d)^m + (y^d)^m = (z^d)^m\). Moreover, any prime element \(\pi \in A\) dividing \(x^d\) and \(y^d\) divides \(x\) and \(y\), contradicting \((x, y) = A\); and \(x^d, y^d, z^d\) cannot all be units in \(A^\times\) unless \(x, y, z \in A^\times\). Hence \((x^d, y^d, z^d)\) is a primitive solution for the exponent \(m\), a contradiction.

Theorem 1 (Fermat’s Theorem for \(n = 4\) over \(\mathbb{Z}\)) The equation \(x^4 + y^4 = z^2\) has no solutions in positive integers \(x, y, z\) with \(\gcd(x, y) = 1\). In particular, \(x^4 + y^4 = z^4\) has no primitive solutions over \(\mathbb{Z}\).

Proof. Suppose toward a contradiction that such triples exist, and choose one minimizing \(z > 0\). Since \(\gcd(x, y) = 1\), \(x\) and \(y\) cannot both be odd, for otherwise \(z^2 = x^4 + y^4 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}\), which is impossible. Without loss of generality, assume \(x\) is odd and \(y\) is even, so \(z\) is odd. Then \((x^2, y^2, z)\) is a primitive Pythagorean triple, meaning \((y^2)^2 = (z - x^2)(z + x^2)\). Since \(z\) and \(x^2\) are coprime odd integers, \(\gcd(z - x^2, z + x^2) = 2\). Moreover, \(z + x^2 \equiv z - x^2 + 2x^2 \equiv z - x^2 + 2 \pmod{4}\), so one of \(\frac{z - x^2}{2}, \frac{z + x^2}{2}\) is odd and the other is even. Since \(y^2\) is even, \(4 \mid y^2\), so \(16 \mid y^4 = (z - x^2)(z + x^2)\). By unique factorization in \(\mathbb{Z}\), there exist coprime positive integers \(u, v\) of opposite parity such that \(z + x^2 = 2u^2\) and \(z - x^2 = 2v^2\) (the reversed assignment would give \(x^2 = v^2 - u^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{4}\) when \(v\) is even and \(u\) is odd, impossible). Subtracting yields \(x^2 = u^2 - v^2\), so \(x^2 + v^2 = u^2\) with \(v\) even (since \(x\) is odd) and \(\gcd(x, v) = 1\), while multiplying gives \(y^2 = 2uv\). Applying the Pythagorean parameterization a second time to \(x^2 + v^2 = u^2\) provides coprime positive integers \(a, b\) such that \(v = 2ab\) and \(u = a^2 + b^2\). Substituting into \(y^2 = 2uv = 4ab(a^2 + b^2)\) shows that \(a, b, a^2 + b^2\) are pairwise coprime positive integers whose product \((y/2)^2\) is a square. Hence \(a = r^2\), \(b = s^2\), and \(a^2 + b^2 = t^2\) for positive integers \(r, s, t\) with \(\gcd(r, s) = 1\). Thus \(r^4 + s^4 = t^2\) with \(t \le t^2 = u < u^2 + v^2 = z\), contradicting the minimality of \(z\).

We now formulate the central algebraic mechanism unifying the \(k[t]\) and \(\mathbb{Z}\) cases. Let \(A\) be a principal ideal domain with fraction field \(F\), let \(p\) be an odd prime (or any integer \(n \ge 3\) when working over \(k[t]\)), and suppose that \(F\) has characteristic not dividing \(p\). Let \(\zeta \in \overline{F}\) be a primitive \(p\)-th root of unity, so that \(\zeta\) is a root of the \(p\)-th cyclotomic polynomial: \[\Phi_p(X) = \frac{X^p - 1}{X - 1} = 1 + X + X^2 + \dots + X^{p-1}\] Let \(K = F(\zeta)\) and let \(B\) be the integral closure of \(A\) in \(K\). Because \(K/F\) is a finite separable extension and \(A\) is a Dedekind domain, \(B\) is again a Dedekind domain.1 In \(B\), the polynomial \(X^p + Y^p\) factors completely into linear forms because \((-1)^p = -1\) for odd \(p\): \[x^p + y^p = \prod_{j=0}^{p-1} (x + \zeta^j y)\] To deduce that each linear factor \((x + \zeta^j y)B\) is close to being the \(p\)-th power of an ideal in \(B\), we must measure the failure of the ideals \((x + \zeta^i y)B\) and \((x + \zeta^j y)B\) to be coprime for \(i \not\equiv j \pmod{p}\). The following proposition shows that their greatest common ideal divisor is controlled entirely by the single element \(\lambda := 1 - \zeta \in B\).

Proposition 2 (Unified Cyclotomic Coprimality and Ideal Factorization) Let \(A\) be a principal ideal domain with fraction field \(F\) of characteristic not dividing an odd prime \(p\). Let \(\zeta \in \overline{F}\) be a primitive \(p\)-th root of unity, let \(B\) be the integral closure of \(A\) in \(K = F(\zeta)\), and set \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta \in B\). Suppose \((x, y, z) \in A^3\) is a primitive solution to \(x^p + y^p = z^p\). Then:

  1. For every \(j \in \{1, \dots, p-1\}\), the element \(\frac{1 - \zeta^j}{1 - \zeta} = 1 + \zeta + \dots + \zeta^{j-1}\) is a unit in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \subset B\), so \((1 - \zeta^j)B = \lambda B\).
  2. For any distinct indices \(0 \le i < j \le p-1\), the sum of principal ideals \((x + \zeta^i y)B + (x + \zeta^j y)B\) divides the principal ideal \(\lambda B\); that is, \(\lambda B \subset (x + \zeta^i y, x + \zeta^j y)B\).
  3. Every prime ideal \(\mathfrak{q} \subset B\) satisfying \(\mathfrak{q} \nmid \lambda B\) divides at most one of the factors \(x + \zeta^j y\) (\(0 \le j \le p-1\)). Consequently, there exist pairwise coprime ideals \(\mathfrak{a}_0, \mathfrak{a}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{a}_{p-1} \subset B\), each coprime to \(\lambda B\), and non-negative integers \(e_{\mathfrak{q}, j} \ge 0\) for the prime ideals \(\mathfrak{q} \mid \lambda B\), such that for each \(0 \le j \le p-1\): \[(x + \zeta^j y)B = \mathfrak{a}_j^p \prod_{\mathfrak{q} \mid \lambda B} \mathfrak{q}^{e_{\mathfrak{q}, j}}\]

Proof. For (1), since \(p\) is prime and \(1 \le j \le p-1\), we have \(\gcd(j, p) = 1\), so there exists an integer \(k \ge 1\) such that \(jk \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\). Then \(\zeta = (\zeta^j)^k\), so both \(\frac{1 - \zeta^j}{1 - \zeta} = \sum_{r=0}^{j-1} \zeta^r\) and its inverse \(\frac{1 - \zeta}{1 - \zeta^j} = \frac{1 - (\zeta^j)^k}{1 - \zeta^j} = \sum_{s=0}^{k-1} \zeta^{js}\) belong to \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \subset B\). Thus \(1 - \zeta^j\) and \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta\) are associates in \(B\).

For (2), let \(\mathfrak{d} = (x + \zeta^i y, x + \zeta^j y)B\) with \(0 \le i < j \le p-1\). Then \(\mathfrak{d}\) contains the difference \((x + \zeta^i y) - (x + \zeta^j y) = \zeta^i(1 - \zeta^{j-i})y\) and the combination \(\zeta^j(x + \zeta^i y) - \zeta^i(x + \zeta^j y) = \zeta^i(\zeta^{j-i} - 1)x\). Since \(\zeta^i \in B^\times\) and \((1 - \zeta^{j-i})B = \lambda B\) by (1), \(\mathfrak{d}\) contains both \(\lambda x B\) and \(\lambda y B\), hence contains \(\lambda(x, y)B\). Because \(x\) and \(y\) are coprime in the principal ideal domain \(A\), there exist \(r, s \in A\) with \(rx + sy = 1\), so \((x, y)B = B\). Therefore \(\lambda B \subset \mathfrak{d}\), meaning \(\mathfrak{d} \mid \lambda B\).

For (3), let \(\mathfrak{q} \subset B\) be any prime ideal with \(\mathfrak{q} \nmid \lambda B\). By (2), \(\mathfrak{q}\) cannot divide \((x + \zeta^i y)B\) and \((x + \zeta^j y)B\) simultaneously for \(i \neq j\). Since \(\prod_{j=0}^{p-1} (x + \zeta^j y)B = (zB)^p\), comparing the discrete valuation \(v_{\mathfrak{q}}\) on both sides yields \(v_{\mathfrak{q}}(x + \zeta^j y) = p\, v_{\mathfrak{q}}(z)\) for the unique \(j\) (if any) such that \(\mathfrak{q} \mid (x + \zeta^j y)B\), and \(v_{\mathfrak{q}}(x + \zeta^i y) = 0\) for all \(i \neq j\). Grouping for each \(j\) all prime powers \(\mathfrak{q}^{v_{\mathfrak{q}}(x + \zeta^j y)/p}\) with \(\mathfrak{q} \nmid \lambda B\) into the ideal \(\mathfrak{a}_j\) gives the stated factorization.

Proposition 2 reveals that the passage from the ideal equation \(\prod_{j=0}^{p-1} (x + \zeta^j y)B = z^p B\) to an element-level equation \(x + \zeta^j y = \alpha_j^p\) in \(B\) is governed by exactly three structural invariants of the Dedekind domain \(B\):

  1. The Ramification of \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta\) in \(B\): Which prime ideals \(\mathfrak{q} \subset B\) divide \(\lambda B\)? Since \(\prod_{j=1}^{p-1}(1 - \zeta^j) = \Phi_p(1) = p\), we have \(\lambda^{p-1} B = p B\), so the primes dividing \(\lambda B\) are precisely the primes of \(B\) lying above \(p A\), and \(p A\) ramifies with ramification index divisible by \(p - 1\) whenever \(p \notin B^\times\).
  2. The \(p\)-Torsion of the Picard Group \(\operatorname{Pic}(\operatorname{Spec} B)[p] = \operatorname{Cl}(B)[p]\): Even when the prime factors of \(\lambda B\) are accounted for so that \((x + \zeta^j y)B = \mathfrak{a}_j^p\), the ideal class \([\mathfrak{a}_j] \in \operatorname{Cl}(B)\) only satisfies \([\mathfrak{a}_j]^p = 1\). For \(\mathfrak{a}_j = \alpha_j B\) to be principal, \(\operatorname{Cl}(B)\) must have trivial \(p\)-torsion.
  3. The Unit Group Quotient \(B^\times / (B^\times)^p\): Once \((x + \zeta^j y)B = (\alpha_j B)^p\), the elements \(x + \zeta^j y\) and \(\alpha_j^p\) still differ by a unit \(u_j \in B^\times\). Only when \(u_j\) can be controlled modulo \((B^\times)^p\) can we eliminate the unit ambiguity and perform a descent.

2 Fermat’s Last Theorem over \(k[t]\) in Characteristic Zero

Let \(k\) be a field of characteristic zero and let \(A = k[t]\). Notice that if \((x, y, z) \in k[t]^3\) is a primitive solution to \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) (\(n \ge 3\)) over \(k[t]\), then \(x, y, z\) remain pairwise coprime in \(\bar{k}[t]\) for an algebraic closure \(\bar{k}\) of \(k\) (since the Euclidean algorithm for \(\gcd(x, y)\) in \(k[t]\) is invariant under field extension) and not all three lie in \(\bar{k}\). Thus we may pass freely between \(k[t]\) and \(\bar{k}[t]\).

We present two algebraic proofs in this section before turning to the geometric proof in Section 3. The first proof uses Proposition 2 and Fermat’s method of infinite descent, deliberately structured to foreshadow Kummer’s Case II descent over \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) in Section 5. The second proof uses Kähler differentials and logarithmic derivatives to establish the Mason–Stothers theorem (Stothers 1981; Mason 1984), which yields an immediate degree bound forbidding solutions for all \(n \ge 3\) (Liouville 1879).

2.1 Cyclotomic Factorization and Polynomial Infinite Descent

Why is the cyclotomic factorization over \(\bar{k}[t]\) so much simpler than over \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\)? Because \(\operatorname{char} k = 0\), the \(n\)-th roots of unity \(\zeta_n^j\) (\(0 \le j \le n-1\)) are distinct constants in \(\bar{k}\), so every difference \(\zeta_n^i - \zeta_n^j\) (\(i \neq j\)) is a nonzero element of the ground field \(\bar{k}\) and hence an invertible unit in \(B = \bar{k}[t]\). Furthermore, the unit group \(\bar{k}[t]^\times = \bar{k}^\times\) is \(n\)-divisible because \(\bar{k}\) is algebraically closed. Thus all three obstructions from Section 1 vanish simultaneously. Once each factor \(z - \zeta_n^j x\) is an \(n\)-th power \(u_j^n\) in \(\bar{k}[t]\), any three of these factors are linearly dependent over \(\bar{k}\) because they are \(\bar{k}\)-linear combinations of the two polynomials \(x\) and \(z\). That linear relation between three \(n\)-th powers \(u_0^n, u_1^n, u_2^n\) of strictly smaller degree drives the infinite descent.

Theorem 2 (Fermat’s Last Theorem over \(k[t]\) via Infinite Descent) Let \(k\) be a field of characteristic zero and let \(n \ge 3\) be an integer. The Fermat equation \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) admits no primitive solutions in \(k[t]\).

Proof. Extending scalars to an algebraic closure \(\bar{k}\) of \(k\), suppose toward a contradiction that primitive solutions \((x, y, z) \in \bar{k}[t]^3\) to \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) exist, and choose one that minimizes the maximum degree \(M = \max(\deg x, \deg y, \deg z) \ge 1\). Rewriting the equation as \(y^n = z^n - x^n\) and fixing a primitive \(n\)-th root of unity \(\zeta \in \bar{k}^\times\) (which exists of exact order \(n\) because \(\operatorname{char} \bar{k} = 0 \nmid n\)), we obtain the factorization in the unique factorization domain \(\bar{k}[t]\): \[y^n = \prod_{j=0}^{n-1} (z - \zeta^j x)\] For any \(0 \le i < j \le n-1\), the difference \(\zeta^i - \zeta^j\) lies in \(\bar{k}^\times = \bar{k}[t]^\times\). Because \((x, z) = \bar{k}[t]\) and both \((\zeta^i - \zeta^j)x = (z - \zeta^j x) - (z - \zeta^i x)\) and \((\zeta^i - \zeta^j)z = \zeta^i(z - \zeta^j x) - \zeta^j(z - \zeta^i x)\) lie in the ideal \((z - \zeta^i x, z - \zeta^j x)\), the factors \(z - \zeta^j x\) (\(0 \le j \le n-1\)) are pairwise coprime in \(\bar{k}[t]\).

Since the product of these pairwise coprime polynomials in the unique factorization domain \(\bar{k}[t]\) is the \(n\)-th power \(y^n\), and every unit \(c \in \bar{k}[t]^\times = \bar{k}^\times\) has an \(n\)-th root in the algebraically closed field \(\bar{k}\), there exist pairwise coprime polynomials \(u_0, u_1, \dots, u_{n-1} \in \bar{k}[t]\) such that \(z - \zeta^j x = u_j^n\) for each \(0 \le j \le n-1\). Because \(n \ge 3\), we may consider the first three factors \(u_0^n = z - x\), \(u_1^n = z - \zeta x\), and \(u_2^n = z - \zeta^2 x\). Eliminating \(z\) and \(x\) via the identity \((\zeta - \zeta^2)(z - x) + (\zeta^2 - 1)(z - \zeta x) + (1 - \zeta)(z - \zeta^2 x) = 0\) yields the three-term linear relation: \[(\zeta - \zeta^2) u_0^n + (\zeta^2 - 1) u_1^n = (\zeta - 1) u_2^n\] Since \(\zeta - \zeta^2, \zeta^2 - 1, \zeta - 1 \in \bar{k}^\times\), we may choose \(n\)-th roots \(\alpha_0, \alpha_1, \alpha_2 \in \bar{k}^\times\) satisfying \(\alpha_0^n = \zeta - \zeta^2\), \(\alpha_1^n = \zeta^2 - 1\), and \(\alpha_2^n = \zeta - 1\). Setting \(\tilde{x} = \alpha_0 u_0\), \(\tilde{y} = \alpha_1 u_1\), and \(\tilde{z} = \alpha_2 u_2\) in \(\bar{k}[t]\), we obtain \(\tilde{x}^n + \tilde{y}^n = \tilde{z}^n\). The polynomials \(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}, \tilde{z}\) are pairwise coprime because \(u_0, u_1, u_2\) are pairwise coprime. They cannot all lie in \(\bar{k}\) because \(x = (1 - \zeta)^{-1}(u_0^n - u_1^n)\) and \(z = u_0^n + x\) would both be constant if \(u_0, u_1 \in \bar{k}\) (and consequently \(y^n = z^n - x^n \in \bar{k}\) would force \(y \in \bar{k}\) as well, contradicting \(M \ge 1\)). Finally, for each \(j \in \{0, 1, 2\}\), we have \(n \deg u_j = \deg(z - \zeta^j x) \le \max(\deg x, \deg z) \le M\), so: \[\max(\deg \tilde{x}, \deg \tilde{y}, \deg \tilde{z}) = \max_{0 \le j \le 2} \deg u_j \le \frac{M}{n} < M\] This contradicts the minimality of \(M\) and completes the proof.

2.2 Kähler Differentials and the Mason–Stothers Theorem

While Theorem 2 relies on factoring \(z^n - x^n\), the deeper reason why high powers of polynomials cannot sum to zero is visible without factoring at all: differentiating a polynomial \(f^n\) with respect to \(t\) produces \(n f^{n-1} f'\), which remains divisible by the high power \(f^{n-1}\). To make this observation coordinate-free and connect it to the geometry of schemes in Section 3, we formulate it using the module of Kähler differentials \(\Omega^1_{\bar{k}[t]/\bar{k}} = \bar{k}[t]\,dt\) and the logarithmic derivative.

Definition 2 (Kähler Differentials and the Logarithmic Derivative) For any commutative \(R\)-algebra \(B\), the module of relative Kähler differentials \(\Omega^1_{B/R}\) is the universal \(B\)-module equipped with an \(R\)-linear derivation \(d \colon B \to \Omega^1_{B/R}\) satisfying the Leibniz rule \(d(fg) = f\,dg + g\,df\) (Hartshorne 1977, ch. II, 8). When \(B = \bar{k}[t]\) over \(R = \bar{k}\), \(\Omega^1_{\bar{k}[t]/\bar{k}}\) is a free \(\bar{k}[t]\)-module of rank one generated by \(dt\), and \(df = f'(t)\,dt\). Passing to the fraction field \(\bar{k}(t)\), the logarithmic derivative is the group homomorphism from the multiplicative group \(\bar{k}(t)^\times\) to the additive group \(\bar{k}(t)\) defined by: \[\operatorname{dlog}(f) := \frac{f'}{f}, \qquad \operatorname{dlog}(fg) = \operatorname{dlog}(f) + \operatorname{dlog}(g)\] For any nonzero polynomial \(f(t) = c \prod_{i=1}^r (t - \alpha_i)^{e_i} \in \bar{k}[t]\) with distinct roots \(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r \in \bar{k}\), define the radical of \(f\) to be the square-free polynomial \(\operatorname{rad}(f) := \prod_{i=1}^r (t - \alpha_i)\), so that \(\deg \operatorname{rad}(f) = r\) counts the number of distinct roots of \(f\) in \(\bar{k}\). By the homomorphism property of \(\operatorname{dlog}\), we have the partial fraction expansion: \[\frac{f'}{f} = \sum_{i=1}^r \frac{e_i}{t - \alpha_i} = \frac{P(t)}{\operatorname{rad}(f)}\] where \(P(t) = \sum_{i=1}^r e_i \prod_{j \neq i} (t - \alpha_j) \in \bar{k}[t]\) has degree strictly less than \(r\). Because \(\operatorname{char} \bar{k} = 0\), the residue \(e_i \neq 0\) at each root \(\alpha_i\), so \(\operatorname{rad}(f) \frac{f'}{f} = P(t)\) belongs to \(\bar{k}[t]\).

Suppose \(a, b, c \in \bar{k}[t]\) are nonzero coprime polynomials satisfying \(a + b = c\). Differentiating gives \(a' + b' = c'\), so the vector \((1, 1, -1)\) lies in the kernel of the \(2 \times 3\) matrix of coefficients and derivatives. Consequently, the determinants of the \(2 \times 2\) minors of this matrix are all equal: \(a b' - a' b = a c' - a' c = b' c - b c'\). Dividing this common Wronskian \(W(a, b) := a b' - a' b\) by \(abc\) expresses it in terms of logarithmic derivatives, where every high multiplicity of a root cancels out and leaves only the radical \(\operatorname{rad}(abc)\) in the denominator.

Theorem 3 (Mason–Stothers Theorem) Let \(k\) be a field of characteristic zero, and let \(a, b, c \in k[t]\) be nonzero pairwise coprime polynomials, not all in \(k\), such that \(a + b = c\). Then: \[\max(\deg a, \deg b, \deg c) \le \deg \operatorname{rad}(abc) - 1\]

Proof. Base-changing to \(\bar{k}[t]\), let \(W = a b' - a' b \in \bar{k}[t]\). First we verify that \(W \neq 0\). If \(W = 0\), then \((b/a)' = W/a^2 = 0\) in \(\bar{k}(t)\). Because \(\operatorname{char} \bar{k} = 0\), any rational function with zero derivative is constant, so \(b = \lambda a\) for some \(\lambda \in \bar{k}^\times\). Since \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\), this forces \(a, b \in \bar{k}^\times\) and hence \(c = a + b \in \bar{k}\), contradicting the hypothesis that \(a, b, c\) are not all constant.

Next, differentiating \(a + b = c\) gives \(a' + b' = c'\), from which \(W = a b' - a' b = a(c' - a') - a'(c - a) = a c' - a' c\). Since \(a, b, c\) are pairwise coprime in \(\bar{k}[t]\), their sets of roots in \(\bar{k}\) are pairwise disjoint, so \(\operatorname{rad}(abc) = \operatorname{rad}(a)\operatorname{rad}(b)\operatorname{rad}(c)\). As shown in Definition 2, the polynomials \(P_a := \operatorname{rad}(a)\frac{a'}{a}\), \(P_b := \operatorname{rad}(b)\frac{b'}{b}\), and \(P_c := \operatorname{rad}(c)\frac{c'}{c}\) all belong to \(\bar{k}[t]\). Rewriting \(W\) in terms of \(P_a, P_b, P_c\) gives: \[W = a b' - a' b = \frac{a}{\operatorname{rad}(a)} \frac{b}{\operatorname{rad}(b)} \bigl(\operatorname{rad}(a) P_b - \operatorname{rad}(b) P_a\bigr)\] and similarly \(W = a c' - a' c = \frac{a}{\operatorname{rad}(a)} \frac{c}{\operatorname{rad}(c)} \bigl(\operatorname{rad}(a) P_c - \operatorname{rad}(c) P_a\bigr)\). Therefore, the three polynomials \(\frac{a}{\operatorname{rad}(a)}\), \(\frac{b}{\operatorname{rad}(b)}\), and \(\frac{c}{\operatorname{rad}(c)}\) each divide \(W\) in \(\bar{k}[t]\). Because \(a, b, c\) are pairwise coprime, these three quotients are pairwise coprime in \(\bar{k}[t]\), so their product \(\frac{abc}{\operatorname{rad}(abc)}\) divides the nonzero polynomial \(W\). Comparing degrees yields: \[\deg a + \deg b + \deg c - \deg \operatorname{rad}(abc) \le \deg W\] Finally, in \(W = a b' - a' b\), both \(a b'\) and \(a' b\) have degree at most \(\deg a + \deg b - 1\), so \(\deg W \le \deg a + \deg b - 1\). Substituting this upper bound into the inequality above and canceling \(\deg a + \deg b\) yields \(\deg c \le \deg \operatorname{rad}(abc) - 1\). By the symmetry of \(a + b + (-c) = 0\), the same bound holds for \(\deg a\) and \(\deg b\).

Corollary 1 (Immediate Deduction of FLT over \(k[t]\) from Mason–Stothers) For any field \(k\) of characteristic zero and any integer \(n \ge 3\), the equation \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) has no primitive solutions in \(k[t]\).

Proof. Suppose \((x, y, z) \in k[t]^3\) is a primitive solution to \(x^n + y^n = z^n\), and set \(M = \max(\deg x, \deg y, \deg z) \ge 1\). Applying Theorem 3 to the pairwise coprime polynomials \(a = x^n\), \(b = y^n\), and \(c = z^n\) yields: \[n M = \max(\deg x^n, \deg y^n, \deg z^n) \le \deg \operatorname{rad}(x^n y^n z^n) - 1 = \deg \operatorname{rad}(xyz) - 1\] Since \(\deg \operatorname{rad}(xyz) \le \deg x + \deg y + \deg z \le 3M\), we obtain \(n M \le 3M - 1\), or \((n - 3)M \le -1\), which is impossible for \(n \ge 3\) and \(M \ge 1\).

3 The Algebro-Geometric Interpretation: The Fermat Curve and Genus

To understand why the Wronskian bound of Theorem 3 is so decisive over \(k[t]\)—and why no elementary analogue exists over \(\mathbb{Z}\)—we pass to the language of schemes and algebraic curves. A primitive solution \((x(t), y(t), z(t)) \in k[t]^3\) to \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) defines for every \(t_0 \in \bar{k}\) a well-defined projective point \([x(t_0) : y(t_0) : z(t_0)]\) on the projective Fermat curve \(F_n \subset \mathbb{P}^2_k\) (since \(x, y, z\) are coprime and thus have no common root in \(\bar{k}\)). Geometrically, this is a non-constant morphism from the projective line \(\mathbb{P}^1_k\) (of genus \(0\)) onto the Fermat curve \(F_n\) (of genus \(g(F_n) = \frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}\)). The Riemann–Hurwitz formula forbids any non-constant morphism from a curve of lower genus to a curve of higher genus.

Remark 2 (Recall on Projective Curves, Smoothness, and Valuative Extension). Let \(k\) be an algebraically closed field (such as \(\bar{k}\)). Recall from (Hartshorne 1977, ch. I, 5–6 and ch. II, 2, 4) the following fundamental facts of scheme theory for curves:

  1. Projective Plane Curves and the Jacobian Criterion: The projective plane over \(k\) is the homogeneous spectrum \(\mathbb{P}^2_k = \operatorname{Proj} k[X, Y, Z]\), covered by the three standard affine open charts \(D_+(X) = \operatorname{Spec} k[Y/X, Z/X]\), \(D_+(Y)\), and \(D_+(Z)\). For a non-constant homogeneous polynomial \(F(X,Y,Z) \in k[X,Y,Z]\) of degree \(n\), the closed subscheme \(C = \operatorname{Proj} k[X,Y,Z]/(F)\) is a smooth projective curve over \(k\) (meaning an integral, separated, regular scheme of dimension \(1\) of finite type over \(k\)) if and only if the partial derivatives \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial X}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial Z}\) have no common zero \([x_0 : y_0 : z_0] \in C(k)\). At every closed point \(P \in C(k)\) of a smooth curve, the local ring \(\mathcal{O}_{C, P}\) is a discrete valuation ring containing \(k\).
  2. Extension of Rational Maps from Smooth Curves to Proper Schemes: Let \(C'\) be a smooth curve over \(k\) with function field \(K(C')\), and let \(Y\) be a projective (hence proper) scheme over \(k\) (such as \(Y = C \subset \mathbb{P}^2_k\)). By the valuative criterion of properness (Hartshorne 1977, ch. II, Theorem 4.7 and Proposition 6.8), any morphism \(U \to Y\) defined on a dense open subscheme \(U \subset C'\) extends uniquely to a morphism \(C' \to Y\) on all of \(C'\). Concretely, if \(x(t), y(t), z(t) \in k[t]\) have \(\gcd(x, y, z) = 1\) and satisfy \(F(x, y, z) = 0\), then on the affine line \(\mathbb{A}^1_k = \operatorname{Spec} k[t] = D_+(T_0) \subset \mathbb{P}^1_k\) (where \(t = T_1/T_0\)), the tuple \([x(t) : y(t) : z(t)]\) has no common zero in \(k\) and defines a morphism \(\mathbb{A}^1_k \to C\). At the point at infinity \(\infty = [0 : 1] \in \mathbb{P}^1_k\) with local uniformizer \(s = t^{-1} \in \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1, \infty} = k[s]_{(s)}\) and \(M = \max(\deg x, \deg y, \deg z)\), multiplying through by \(s^M\) yields \([s^M x(s^{-1}) : s^M y(s^{-1}) : s^M z(s^{-1})]\), where at least one coordinate is nonzero at \(s = 0\). Thus \([x : y : z]\) extends uniquely to a morphism \(\pi \colon \mathbb{P}^1_k \to C\) of degree \(M\).

To compute the genus of the Fermat curve \(F_n\) and apply the Riemann–Hurwitz obstruction without circularity, we recall in the side note how the genus \(g(C)\) of a smooth projective curve \(C\) is defined via the canonical sheaf of Kähler differentials \(\Omega^1_{C/\bar{k}}\) and how it transforms under a finite separable morphism \(\pi \colon C' \to C\).

Theorem 4 (Canonical Differentials and the Riemann–Hurwitz Formula) Let \(C\) be a smooth projective curve over an algebraically closed field \(\bar{k}\) of characteristic zero. The sheaf of relative Kähler differentials \(\Omega^1_{C/\bar{k}}\) is an invertible \(\mathcal{O}_C\)-module (a line bundle), called the canonical sheaf \(\omega_C\) (Hartshorne 1977, ch. II, Theorem 8.15). Its space of global sections \(H^0(C, \Omega^1_{C/\bar{k}})\) has finite dimension \(g(C) := \dim_{\bar{k}} H^0(C, \Omega^1_{C/\bar{k}})\), called the genus of \(C\), and by the Riemann–Roch theorem its degree is \(\deg \Omega^1_{C/\bar{k}} = 2g(C) - 2\). For \(\mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\) with affine coordinate \(t\), the differential \(dt\) has no zeros or poles on \(\mathbb{A}^1_{\bar{k}}\), while at \(\infty\) with uniformizer \(s = t^{-1}\) we have \(dt = d(s^{-1}) = -s^{-2}\,ds\), which has a pole of order \(2\); thus \(\deg \Omega^1_{\mathbb{P}^1/\bar{k}} = -2\) and \(g(\mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}) = 0\).

Now let \(\pi \colon C' \to C\) be a non-constant (hence finite surjective) morphism of smooth projective curves over \(\bar{k}\) of degree \(d = [K(C') : \pi^* K(C)]\). At each closed point \(P \in C'(\bar{k})\) mapping to \(Q = \pi(P) \in C(\bar{k})\), choosing uniformizers \(u_P \in \mathcal{O}_{C', P}\) and \(u_Q \in \mathcal{O}_{C, Q}\) allows us to write \(\pi^*(u_Q) = \varepsilon u_P^{e_P}\) with \(\varepsilon \in \mathcal{O}_{C', P}^\times\), where \(e_P = v_P(\pi^* u_Q) \ge 1\) is the ramification index of \(\pi\) at \(P\). Pulling back differentials gives \(\pi^*(du_Q) = d(\varepsilon u_P^{e_P}) = (e_P \varepsilon u_P^{e_P - 1} + u_P^{e_P} \frac{d\varepsilon}{du_P})\,du_P\). Because \(\operatorname{char} \bar{k} = 0\), \(e_P \neq 0\) in \(\bar{k}\), so \(\pi^*(du_Q)\) vanishes to exact order \(e_P - 1\) relative to the local generator \(du_P\) of \(\Omega^1_{C'/\bar{k}}\). Globally, this defines the short exact sequence of sheaves on \(C'\): \[0 \longrightarrow \pi^* \Omega^1_{C/\bar{k}} \longrightarrow \Omega^1_{C'/\bar{k}} \longrightarrow \Omega^1_{C'/C} \longrightarrow 0\] where the relative differential sheaf \(\Omega^1_{C'/C}\) is a skyscraper sheaf supported at the ramification points with length \(e_P - 1\) at \(P\). Taking degrees of the invertible sheaves \(\Omega^1_{C'/\bar{k}} \cong \pi^* \Omega^1_{C/\bar{k}} \otimes \mathcal{O}_{C'}(R_\pi)\), where \(R_\pi = \sum_{P \in C'(\bar{k})} (e_P - 1)[P]\) is the ramification divisor, yields the Riemann–Hurwitz formula (Hartshorne 1977, ch. IV, Corollary 2.4): \[2g(C') - 2 = (\deg \pi)\bigl(2g(C) - 2\bigr) + \sum_{P \in C'(\bar{k})} (e_P - 1)\]

Using Theorem 4, we first verify that the Fermat curve \(F_n\) is smooth and compute its genus by projecting onto \(\mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\).

Proposition 3 (Smoothness and Genus of the Projective Fermat Curve) Let \(\bar{k}\) be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and let \(n \ge 1\) be an integer. The projective Fermat curve: \[F_n := \operatorname{Proj} \bar{k}[X, Y, Z] / (X^n + Y^n - Z^n) \subset \mathbb{P}^2_{\bar{k}}\] is a smooth projective curve over \(\bar{k}\) of genus: \[g(F_n) = \frac{(n - 1)(n - 2)}{2}\]

Proof. For \(F(X,Y,Z) = X^n + Y^n - Z^n\), the partial derivatives are \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial X} = n X^{n-1}\), \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y} = n Y^{n-1}\), and \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial Z} = -n Z^{n-1}\). Because \(\operatorname{char} \bar{k} = 0\), \(n \neq 0\) in \(\bar{k}\), so these three partial derivatives vanish simultaneously only at \((X, Y, Z) = (0, 0, 0)\), which does not define a point of \(\mathbb{P}^2_{\bar{k}}\). Since \(X^n + Y^n - Z^n\) has no singular points, it is also irreducible (as any intersection point of two components in \(\mathbb{P}^2_{\bar{k}}\) would be a singular point by Bézout’s theorem). By the Jacobian criterion (Remark 2), \(F_n\) is a smooth projective curve over \(\bar{k}\).

To compute \(g(F_n)\), consider the projection morphism \(\rho \colon F_n \to \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\) defined by \([X : Y : Z] \mapsto [X : Z]\) (which is well-defined everywhere on \(F_n\) because \(X = Z = 0\) forces \(Y^n = 0\), hence \(Y = 0\)). On the affine chart \(Z = 1\) with coordinates \(x = X/Z\) and \(y = Y/Z\), the curve \(F_n\) is given by \(y^n = 1 - x^n\), so the function field extension \(\bar{k}(F_n)/\rho^*\bar{k}(\mathbb{P}^1) = \bar{k}(x)[y]/(y^n - (1 - x^n))\) has degree \(\deg \rho = n\).

Let us determine the ramification indices \(e_P\) of \(\rho\). Over the point at infinity \([1 : 0] \in \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\) (where \(Z = 0\)), the fiber on \(F_n\) consists of the \(n\) distinct points \([1 : \zeta_n^j \sqrt[n]{-1} : 0]\) (\(0 \le j \le n-1\)), so \(\rho\) is unramified over \([1 : 0]\) (since \(\sum_{P \mapsto Q} e_P = \deg \rho = n\)). Over an affine point \([x_0 : 1] \in \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\), if \(x_0^n \neq 1\), then \(y^n = 1 - x_0^n \neq 0\) again has \(n\) distinct roots in \(\bar{k}\), so \(\rho\) is unramified over \([x_0 : 1]\). Finally, over each of the \(n\) points \(Q_j = [\zeta_n^j : 1] \in \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\) (\(0 \le j \le n-1\)) where \(x_0^n = 1\), the fiber consists of the single point \(P_j = [\zeta_n^j : 0 : 1] \in F_n(\bar{k})\) with \(e_{P_j} = n\). Applying the Riemann–Hurwitz formula (Theorem 4) to \(\rho \colon F_n \to \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\) with \(g(\mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}) = 0\) and \(\deg \rho = n\) gives: \[2g(F_n) - 2 = n(2 \cdot 0 - 2) + \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (n - 1) = -2n + n(n - 1) = n^2 - 3n\] Adding \(2\) to both sides yields \(2g(F_n) = n^2 - 3n + 2 = (n - 1)(n - 2)\), as claimed.

We can now give the third, purely geometric proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem over \(k[t]\), and explain why the Mason–Stothers inequality is the exact coordinate manifestation of the Riemann–Hurwitz formula for a map \(\pi \colon \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}} \to \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\).

Theorem 5 (Geometric Proof of FLT over \(k[t]\) via the Riemann–Hurwitz Formula) Let \(k\) be a field of characteristic zero and let \(n \ge 3\) be an integer. There is no non-constant morphism \(\pi \colon \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}} \to F_n\), and consequently no primitive solution to \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) in \(k[t]\).

Proof. Suppose \((x, y, z) \in k[t]^3\) is a primitive solution to \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) with \(M = \max(\deg x, \deg y, \deg z) \ge 1\). As shown in Remark 2 (2), the map \(t \mapsto [x(t) : y(t) : z(t)]\) extends uniquely to a non-constant morphism of smooth projective curves \(\pi \colon \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}} \to F_n\) of degree \(d = M \ge 1\). Applying the Riemann–Hurwitz formula (Theorem 4) to \(\pi \colon \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}} \to F_n\) and substituting \(g(\mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}) = 0\) and \(2g(F_n) - 2 = n^2 - 3n\) from Proposition 3 yields: \[-2 = 2g(\mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}) - 2 = d\bigl(2g(F_n) - 2\bigr) + \sum_{P \in \mathbb{P}^1(\bar{k})} (e_P - 1) \ge d(n^2 - 3n)\] because \(e_P \ge 1\) for every \(P \in \mathbb{P}^1(\bar{k})\). However, for \(n \ge 3\) and \(d \ge 1\), we have \(d(n^2 - 3n) = dn(n - 3) \ge 0 > -2\), a contradiction.

Remark 3 (Why Mason–Stothers and Riemann–Hurwitz Are the Same Proof). Consider any three pairwise coprime polynomials \(a, b, c \in \bar{k}[t]\) satisfying \(a + b = c\) with \(M = \max(\deg a, \deg b, \deg c) \ge 1\), and form the finite morphism \(\phi \colon \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}} \to \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\) given by \(t \mapsto [a(t) : c(t)]\), which has degree \(M\). Over the three points \(0 = [0:1]\), \(1 = [1:1]\), and \(\infty = [1:0]\) of the target \(\mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\), the preimages \(\phi^{-1}(\{0, 1, \infty\})\) consist of the roots of \(a\), \(b = c - a\), and \(c\) in \(\mathbb{A}^1_{\bar{k}}\) together with the point at infinity \(\infty \in \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\). Thus the total number of distinct points in \(\phi^{-1}(\{0, 1, \infty\})\) is at most \(\deg \operatorname{rad}(abc) + 1\). Since \(\sum_{P \in \phi^{-1}(Q)} e_P = M\) for each \(Q \in \{0, 1, \infty\}\), summing \(e_P - 1\) over \(S = \phi^{-1}(\{0, 1, \infty\})\) gives: \[\sum_{P \in S} (e_P - 1) = 3M - |S| \ge 3M - \deg \operatorname{rad}(abc) - 1\] On the other hand, applying the Riemann–Hurwitz formula to \(\phi \colon \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}} \to \mathbb{P}^1_{\bar{k}}\) gives \(-2 = M(-2) + \sum_{P \in \mathbb{P}^1} (e_P - 1) \ge -2M + \sum_{P \in S} (e_P - 1)\). Combining the two inequalities yields \(2M - 2 \ge 3M - \deg \operatorname{rad}(abc) - 1\), or \(M \le \deg \operatorname{rad}(abc) - 1\), which recovers Theorem 3.

Remark 4 (Scheme-Theoretic Contrast: Why \(\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}\) Needs Cyclotomic Covers). Over \(\mathbb{Z}\), the Fermat equation defines a projective scheme \(\mathcal{F}_p = \operatorname{Proj} \mathbb{Z}[X,Y,Z]/(X^p + Y^p - Z^p)\) over \(\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}\) (an arithmetic surface). By the valuative criterion of properness over the Dedekind domain \(\mathbb{Z}\), primitive integer solutions \((x, y, z) \in \mathbb{Z}^3\) correspond bijectively to sections \(\sigma \colon \operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathcal{F}_p\) of the structural morphism \(\mathcal{F}_p \to \operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}\). Unlike \(\mathbb{P}^1_k \to \operatorname{Spec} k\), however, \(\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}\) does not lie over a base field \(k\): the absolute Kähler differential module \(\Omega^1_{\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}} = 0\) vanishes identically, so there is no global derivation \(d/dt\) to annihilate \(p\)-th powers. Instead, taking the base change along the finite flat morphism \(\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] \to \operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}\) (which is étale away from \((p)\) and totally ramified over \((p)\), where \(\Omega^1_{\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]/\mathbb{Z}} \cong \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]/(\lambda^{p-2})\)) splits the affine cone of \(\mathcal{F}_p\) into the \(p\) hyperplanes \(X + \zeta_p^j Y = \alpha_j^p\) up to the Picard group \(\operatorname{Pic}(\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p])\) and units \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\).

4 Arithmetic of the Cyclotomic Field \(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)\) from First Principles

We now turn to the arithmetic case \(A = \mathbb{Z}\). Fix an odd prime \(p\), let \(\zeta_p = e^{2\pi i / p} \in \mathbb{C}\) be a primitive \(p\)-th root of unity, and set \(K = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)\) and \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta_p\). Rather than quoting specialized theorems on cyclotomic fields, we derive the three foundational properties of \(K\) needed for Kummer’s proof—its prime ramification at \(p\), its ring of integers \(\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\), and the structure of its unit group \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\)—directly from standard Dedekind domain and ramification theory (Neukirch 1999, ch. I, 2, 8).

Remark 5 (Recall on Ramification, Norms, and Discriminants in Dedekind Domains). Let \(A\) be a Dedekind domain with fraction field \(F\), let \(K/F\) be a finite separable extension of degree \(d = [K:F]\), and let \(B\) be the integral closure of \(A\) in \(K\). Recall (Neukirch 1999, ch. I, Proposition 8.2 and 2) the following properties:

  1. Fundamental Ramification Identity: Every nonzero prime ideal \(\mathfrak{p}_0 \subset A\) factors uniquely in \(B\) as \(\mathfrak{p}_0 B = \mathfrak{p}_1^{e_1} \cdots \mathfrak{p}_g^{e_g}\), where \(e_i = e(\mathfrak{p}_i/\mathfrak{p}_0) \ge 1\) is the ramification index and \(f_i = [B/\mathfrak{p}_i : A/\mathfrak{p}_0] \ge 1\) is the residue degree, satisfying: \[\sum_{i=1}^g e_i f_i = [K : F] = d\] In particular, if we exhibit a nonzero element \(\lambda \in B\) such that \(\mathfrak{p}_0 B = \lambda^d B\), then \(\mathfrak{p}_0 B\) must be the \(d\)-th power of a single prime ideal \(\mathfrak{p} = \lambda B\) with \(g = 1\), \(e_1 = d\), and \(f_1 = 1\), so \(B/\lambda B \cong A/\mathfrak{p}_0\).
  2. Power-Basis Discriminant Formula: For any primitive element \(\theta \in B\) with minimal polynomial \(f(X) \in A[X]\) of degree \(d\), the discriminant of the \(F\)-basis \((1, \theta, \dots, \theta^{d-1})\) is: \[\operatorname{disc}(1, \theta, \dots, \theta^{d-1}) := \det\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/F}(\theta^{i+j})\bigr)_{0 \le i,j < d} = \prod_{1 \le i < j \le d} (\theta_i - \theta_j)^2 = (-1)^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}} N_{K/F}\bigl(f'(\theta)\bigr)\] Moreover, if \(A = \mathbb{Z}\) and \(B = \mathcal{O}_K\) is a free \(\mathbb{Z}\)-module of rank \(d\), then for any full-rank subring \(\mathbb{Z}[\theta] \subset \mathcal{O}_K\) of finite index \(m = [\mathcal{O}_K : \mathbb{Z}[\theta]]\), their discriminants are related by \(\operatorname{disc}(1, \theta, \dots, \theta^{d-1}) = m^2 \operatorname{disc}(\mathcal{O}_K)\). In particular, any prime dividing \([\mathcal{O}_K : \mathbb{Z}[\theta]]\) must have its square dividing \(\operatorname{disc}(1, \theta, \dots, \theta^{d-1})\).

4.1 Ramification of \(p\) and the Ring of Integers \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\)

Our first step is to determine the degree \([K : \mathbb{Q}]\), the factorization of the rational prime \((p)\) in \(\mathcal{O}_K\), and the discriminant of the subring \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\). The identity \(\Phi_p(1) = p\) immediately forces \((p) = (\lambda)^{p-1}\), which leaves no room in the identity \(\sum e_i f_i = p - 1\) for anything other than total ramification.

Proposition 4 (Irreducibility of \(\Phi_p(X)\), Total Ramification of \(p\), and Discriminant) Let \(p\) be an odd prime, \(\zeta_p = e^{2\pi i / p}\), \(K = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)\), and \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta_p\). Then:

  1. The cyclotomic polynomial \(\Phi_p(X) = 1 + X + \dots + X^{p-1}\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb{Q}\), so \([K : \mathbb{Q}] = p - 1\) and \(\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times\) via \(\sigma_a(\zeta_p) = \zeta_p^a\) for \((a, p) = 1\).
  2. In the ring of integers \(\mathcal{O}_K\), the principal ideal \(\mathfrak{p} := \lambda \mathcal{O}_K\) is the unique prime ideal lying above \(p\), with \((p)\mathcal{O}_K = \mathfrak{p}^{p-1}\), \(e(\mathfrak{p}/p) = p - 1\), \(f(\mathfrak{p}/p) = 1\), \(\mathcal{O}_K / \mathfrak{p} \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\), and \(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda) = p\).
  3. The discriminant of the power basis \((1, \zeta_p, \dots, \zeta_p^{p-2})\) of \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) is: \[\operatorname{disc}(1, \zeta_p, \dots, \zeta_p^{p-2}) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}} p^{p-2}\]

Proof. For (1), substitute \(X = Y + 1\) into \(\Phi_p(X) = \frac{X^p - 1}{X - 1}\): \[\Phi_p(Y + 1) = \frac{(Y + 1)^p - 1}{Y} = Y^{p-1} + \binom{p}{1}Y^{p-2} + \dots + \binom{p}{p-2}Y + p\] Since \(p\) is prime, \(p \mid \binom{p}{j}\) for all \(1 \le j \le p-1\) and \(p^2 \nmid p\). By Eisenstein’s criterion, \(\Phi_p(Y + 1)\) is irreducible in \(\mathbb{Q}[Y]\), hence \(\Phi_p(X)\) is irreducible in \(\mathbb{Q}[X]\) and \([K : \mathbb{Q}] = \deg \Phi_p = p - 1\). The \(p - 1\) distinct roots \(\zeta_p^a\) (\(1 \le a \le p-1\)) are permuted transitively by \(\sigma_a \in \operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\).

For (2), evaluating \(\Phi_p(X) = \prod_{j=1}^{p-1} (X - \zeta_p^j)\) at \(X = 1\) gives \(p = \prod_{j=1}^{p-1} (1 - \zeta_p^j) = N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda)\). By Proposition 2 (1), each \(1 - \zeta_p^j\) is an associate of \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta_p\) in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] \subset \mathcal{O}_K\). Hence \((p)\mathcal{O}_K = \lambda^{p-1} \mathcal{O}_K\). Since \([K : \mathbb{Q}] = p - 1\) and \(\lambda\) cannot be a unit in \(\mathcal{O}_K\) (as \(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda) = p \neq \pm 1\)), the fundamental identity \(\sum_{i=1}^g e_i f_i = p - 1\) (Remark 5) implies that \(\mathfrak{p} = \lambda \mathcal{O}_K\) is a prime ideal with \(e(\mathfrak{p}/p) = p - 1\) and \(f(\mathfrak{p}/p) = 1\), so \(\mathcal{O}_K/\mathfrak{p} \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\).

For (3), differentiating the identity \(X^p - 1 = (X - 1)\Phi_p(X)\) gives \(p X^{p-1} = \Phi_p(X) + (X - 1)\Phi_p'(X)\). Evaluating at \(X = \zeta_p\) (where \(\Phi_p(\zeta_p) = 0\)) yields \(-\lambda \Phi_p'(\zeta_p) = p \zeta_p^{p-1}\). Since \(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_p) = (-1)^{p-1} \Phi_p(0) = 1\), \(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(-\lambda) = (-1)^{p-1} p = p\), and \(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(p) = p^{p-1}\), taking norms from \(K\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\) gives \(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\Phi_p'(\zeta_p)) = p^{p-2}\). Applying the discriminant formula of Remark 5 with \(d = p - 1\) (and noting \(\frac{(p-1)(p-2)}{2} \equiv \frac{p-1}{2} \pmod{2}\) since \(p - 2\) is odd) gives \(\operatorname{disc}(1, \zeta_p, \dots, \zeta_p^{p-2}) = (-1)^{(p-1)/2} p^{p-2}\).

Having computed \(\operatorname{disc}(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]) = \pm p^{p-2}\) and \(\mathcal{O}_K / \lambda \mathcal{O}_K \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\), we can now prove that \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) is the entire ring of integers \(\mathcal{O}_K\). Because \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] = \mathbb{Z}[\lambda]\), the powers \(1, \lambda, \dots, \lambda^{p-2}\) form a \(\mathbb{Z}\)-basis of \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) that is adapted to the \(\mathfrak{p}\)-adic filtration of \(\mathcal{O}_K\).

Theorem 6 (Ring of Integers of \(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)\)) For every odd prime \(p\), the ring of integers of \(K = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)\) is \(\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] = \mathbb{Z}[\lambda]\).

Proof. Let \(m = [\mathcal{O}_K : \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]]\) be the index of the full-rank sublattice \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] = \mathbb{Z}[\lambda]\) inside \(\mathcal{O}_K\). By Remark 5 (2) and Proposition 4 (3), \(m^2\) divides \(\operatorname{disc}(1, \zeta_p, \dots, \zeta_p^{p-2}) = \pm p^{p-2}\), so any prime divisor of \(m\) must be \(p\).

Suppose toward a contradiction that \(p \mid m\). By Cauchy’s theorem applied to the finite abelian group \(\mathcal{O}_K / \mathbb{Z}[\lambda]\), there exists an element \(\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K \setminus \mathbb{Z}[\lambda]\) such that \(p\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[\lambda]\). Write \(p\alpha = \sum_{j=0}^{p-2} c_j \lambda^j\) with \(c_0, \dots, c_{p-2} \in \mathbb{Z}\), and let \(r \in \{0, \dots, p-2\}\) be the smallest index such that \(p \nmid c_r\) (such an \(r\) exists because otherwise every \(c_j / p \in \mathbb{Z}\), which would give \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[\lambda]\)). Subtracting the algebraic integer \(\sum_{j=0}^{r-1} (c_j/p)\lambda^j \in \mathbb{Z}[\lambda]\) from \(\alpha\), we may assume that \(c_0 = \dots = c_{r-1} = 0\), so that: \[p\alpha = c_r \lambda^r + c_{r+1}\lambda^{r+1} + \dots + c_{p-2}\lambda^{p-2}\] In \(\mathcal{O}_K\), we have \((p)\mathcal{O}_K = \lambda^{p-1}\mathcal{O}_K\) by Proposition 4 (2), so \(\lambda^{p-1}\) divides \(p\alpha\) in \(\mathcal{O}_K\). Since \(r \le p - 2\), \(\lambda^{r+1}\) divides both \(p\alpha\) and \(\sum_{j=r+1}^{p-2} c_j \lambda^j\) in \(\mathcal{O}_K\), and therefore \(\lambda^{r+1}\) divides \(c_r \lambda^r\) in \(\mathcal{O}_K\). Canceling \(\lambda^r\) shows that \(c_r \in \lambda \mathcal{O}_K = \mathfrak{p}\). Because \(\mathfrak{p} \cap \mathbb{Z} = p\mathbb{Z}\), this forces \(p \mid c_r\), contradicting our choice of \(r\). Thus \(m = 1\) and \(\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\).

4.2 Structure of Cyclotomic Units and Congruences Modulo \(p\)

In \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\), the unit group \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) is infinite whenever \(p \ge 5\). Fortunately, complex conjugation \(\tau = \sigma_{-1} \in \operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\), defined by \(\bar{\zeta}_p = \zeta_p^{-1}\), belongs to the center of the abelian Galois group \(\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\). Consequently, for any unit \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\), the quotient \(u/\bar{u}\) has absolute value \(1\) under every complex embedding of \(K\). A classical lemma of Kronecker implies that \(u/\bar{u}\) is a root of unity in \(K\), which allows us to factor every unit in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) as a power of \(\zeta_p\) times a real unit \(w = \bar{w}\).

Lemma 1 (Kronecker’s Lemma on Algebraic Integers on the Unit Circle) Let \(\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K\) be a nonzero algebraic integer in a number field \(K\) of degree \(d = [K : \mathbb{Q}]\). If \(|\sigma(\alpha)| = 1\) for every field embedding \(\sigma \colon K \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}\), then \(\alpha\) is a root of unity.

Proof. For every integer \(m \ge 1\), the power \(\alpha^m\) lies in \(\mathcal{O}_K\) and hence is a root of its characteristic polynomial \(P_m(X) = \prod_{\sigma \colon K \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}} (X - \sigma(\alpha^m)) \in \mathbb{Z}[X]\), which is a monic polynomial of degree \(d\) with rational integer coefficients. Writing \(P_m(X) = X^d + \sum_{k=1}^d (-1)^k s_k X^{d-k}\), the \(k\)-th elementary symmetric polynomial \(s_k \in \mathbb{Z}\) satisfies \(|s_k| \le \binom{d}{k} \max_{\sigma} |\sigma(\alpha)|^m = \binom{d}{k}\) by the triangle inequality. Because there are only finitely many integer tuples \((s_1, \dots, s_d) \in \mathbb{Z}^d\) satisfying \(|s_k| \le \binom{d}{k}\), the set of polynomials \(\{P_m(X) \mid m \ge 1\}\) is finite, and thus the set of their roots \(\{\alpha^m \mid m \ge 1\}\) is finite. Hence \(\alpha^m = \alpha^n\) for some \(1 \le m < n\), so \(\alpha^{n-m} = 1\).

Proposition 5 (Real-Unit Decomposition in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\)) Let \(p\) be an odd prime and \(K = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)\).

  1. Every root of unity in \(K\) is of the form \(\pm \zeta_p^a\) for some \(a \in \mathbb{Z}\).
  2. For every unit \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\), there exists a unique residue class \(r \in \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\) and a real unit \(w \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p + \zeta_p^{-1}]^\times\) (meaning \(\bar{w} = w\)) such that \(u = \zeta_p^r w\).

Proof. For (1), suppose \(\omega \in \mathcal{O}_K\) is a root of unity of order \(m\). Replace \(\omega\) by \(-\omega\) if \(2 \mid m\) and \(4 \nmid m\), and if \(2^k \mid m\) with \(k \ge 2\), then \(i = \zeta_4 \in K\), so \(2 = (1 + i)(1 - i)\) ramifies in \(K\), which is impossible because \(2 \nmid \operatorname{disc}(\mathcal{O}_K) = \pm p^{p-2}\). Similarly, if an odd prime \(q \neq p\) divided \(m\), then \(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_q) \subset K\) would force \(q \mid \operatorname{disc}(\mathcal{O}_K) = \pm p^{p-2}\), a contradiction. Finally, if \(p^2 \mid m\), then \([\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^2}) : \mathbb{Q}] = p(p-1) > p - 1 = [K : \mathbb{Q}]\), impossible. Thus \(m \mid 2p\), so \(\omega = \pm \zeta_p^a\).

For (2), let \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\). Because \(\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\) is abelian, every embedding \(\sigma \colon K \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}\) commutes with complex conjugation: \(\sigma(\bar{u}) = \overline{\sigma(u)}\). Therefore \(|\sigma(u/\bar{u})| = |\sigma(u)| / |\overline{\sigma(u)}| = 1\) for every \(\sigma\). By Lemma 1 and part (1), \(u/\bar{u} = \pm \zeta_p^a\) for some \(a \in \mathbb{Z}\).

We claim that the sign must be \(+1\). Since \(\mathcal{O}_K / \lambda \mathcal{O}_K \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\), there exists \(c \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(u \equiv c \pmod{\lambda}\). Taking complex conjugates and noting that \(\bar{\lambda} = 1 - \zeta_p^{-1} = -\zeta_p^{-1}\lambda \in \lambda \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\), we obtain \(\bar{u} \equiv c \equiv u \pmod{\lambda}\). Also \(\zeta_p = 1 - \lambda \equiv 1 \pmod{\lambda}\), so \(\zeta_p^a \equiv 1 \pmod{\lambda}\). If \(u = -\zeta_p^a \bar{u}\), reducing modulo \(\lambda\) would give \(u \equiv -u \pmod{\lambda}\), so \(\lambda \mid 2u\). Because \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) is a unit, this would imply \(\lambda \mid 2\), so \(p = N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda) \mid N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(2) = 2^{p-1}\), contradicting that \(p\) is an odd prime. Hence \(u/\bar{u} = +\zeta_p^a\). Since \(p\) is odd, \(2\) is invertible modulo \(p\); choosing \(r \in \mathbb{Z}\) with \(2r \equiv a \pmod{p}\) and setting \(w = \zeta_p^{-r} u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) gives \(\bar{w} = \zeta_p^r \bar{u} = \zeta_p^{r-a} u = \zeta_p^{-r} u = w\), as required.

Finally, we establish two elementary lemmas on \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) modulo \(p\) that drive the congruence arguments in both Case I and Case II of Kummer’s theorem: first, that the \(p\)-th power of any cyclotomic integer \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) is congruent to a rational integer modulo \(\lambda^p\) (and hence modulo \(p\), since \((p) = (\lambda)^{p-1}\)); and second, a criterion for when a small linear combination of powers of \(\zeta_p\) vanishes modulo \(p\).

Lemma 2 (Rational Congruence of \(p\)-th Powers and Basis Independence Modulo \(p\)) Let \(p\) be an odd prime and \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta_p \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\).

  1. For every \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\), there exists a rational integer \(a \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(\alpha^p \equiv a^p \equiv a \pmod{\lambda^p \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]}\), and in particular \(\alpha^p \equiv a \pmod{p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]}\).
  2. Because \(\{1, \zeta_p, \dots, \zeta_p^{p-2}\}\) is a \(\mathbb{Z}\)-basis of \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\), an element \(\sum_{j=0}^{p-2} c_j \zeta_p^j\) (\(c_j \in \mathbb{Z}\)) belongs to \(p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) if and only if \(p \mid c_j\) for all \(0 \le j \le p - 2\).

Proof. For (1), since \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] / \lambda \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\), we can write \(\alpha = a + \lambda \beta\) with \(a \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(\beta \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\). Expanding \((a + \lambda\beta)^p\) by the binomial theorem gives \(\alpha^p = a^p + \sum_{k=1}^{p-1} \binom{p}{k} a^{p-k} \lambda^k \beta^k + \lambda^p \beta^p\). Since \(p \mid \binom{p}{k}\) for \(1 \le k \le p-1\) and \((p) = (\lambda)^{p-1}\), each intermediate term \(\binom{p}{k} a^{p-k} \lambda^k \beta^k\) for \(1 \le k \le p-1\) is divisible by \(p\lambda\), hence divisible by \(\lambda^p\). Therefore \(\alpha^p \equiv a^p \pmod{\lambda^p}\), and since \(a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}\) with \(p \in (\lambda^{p-1})\), we also have \(\alpha^p \equiv a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}\).

For (2), by Theorem 6, \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) is the free \(\mathbb{Z}\)-module with basis \(\{1, \zeta_p, \dots, \zeta_p^{p-2}\}\), so \(p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] = \bigoplus_{j=0}^{p-2} p\mathbb{Z}\zeta_p^j\).

5 Fermat’s Last Theorem over \(\mathbb{Z}\) for Regular Primes

We are now ready to synthesize the Dedekind domain factorization of Section 1 with the cyclotomic arithmetic of Section 4 to prove Kummer’s landmark theorem (Kummer 1847, 1850). Recall that for any Dedekind domain \(B\) of a number field, the ideal class group \(\operatorname{Cl}(B) = \operatorname{Pic}(\operatorname{Spec} B)\) is finite (Neukirch 1999, ch. I, Theorem 6.3).

Definition 3 (Regular Primes) An odd prime \(p\) is called regular if \(p\) does not divide the class number \(h_p := |\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p])|\) of the \(p\)-th cyclotomic field \(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)\). Equivalently, the finite abelian group \(\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p])\) has no nontrivial \(p\)-torsion: for any fractional ideal \(\mathfrak{a}\) of \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\), if \(\mathfrak{a}^p\) is principal, then \(\mathfrak{a}\) itself is principal.

Remark 6 (Kummer’s Bernoulli Criterion and Irregular Primes). Why did Kummer introduce regularity? In 1850, Kummer (Kummer 1850; Washington 1997, Theorem 5.34) proved a remarkable numerical criterion connecting the algebraic condition \(p \nmid h_p\) to the rational Bernoulli numbers \(B_{2k} \in \mathbb{Q}\), defined by the generating function: \[\frac{t}{e^t - 1} = \sum_{m=0}^\infty B_m \frac{t^m}{m!}\] Specifically, an odd prime \(p\) is regular if and only if \(p\) does not divide the numerator of any of the even Bernoulli numbers \(B_2, B_4, \dots, B_{p-3}\). Among the odd primes \(p < 100\), all primes are regular except for the three irregular primes \(p = 37, 59, 67\) (for instance, \(37 \mid \operatorname{num}(B_{32})\)). While K. L. Jensen proved in 1915 that there are infinitely many irregular primes, it remains an open problem whether there are infinitely many regular primes (heuristically, a fraction \(e^{-1/2} \approx 60.65\%\) of all primes are regular).

Look back at Proposition 2: for a primitive solution \((x, y, z) \in \mathbb{Z}^3\) to \(x^p + y^p = z^p\) (which for odd \(p\) we may write symmetrically as \(x^p + y^p + z^p = 0\) by replacing \(z\) with \(-z\)), the only prime ideal of \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) that can divide more than one of the factors \(x + \zeta_p^j y\) (\(0 \le j \le p-1\)) is the ramified prime \(\mathfrak{p} = \lambda \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) above \(p\). Moreover, since \(\zeta_p \equiv 1 \pmod{\lambda}\), all \(p\) factors are congruent to one another modulo \(\lambda\): \[x + \zeta_p^j y \equiv x + y \pmod{\lambda}\] Therefore, \(\lambda\) divides \(x + \zeta_p^j y\) for some \(j\) if and only if \(\lambda\) divides \(x + \zeta_p^j y\) for every \(j\), which happens if and only if \(p = N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda)\) divides \(\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}(x + \zeta_p^j y) = x^p + y^p = -z^p\), i.e., \(p \mid z\). Thus the proof splits cleanly into two cases:

  • Case I (\(p \nmid xyz\)): None of the factors \(x + \zeta_p^j y\) is divisible by \(\lambda\), so the principal ideals \((x + \zeta_p^j y)\) are strictly pairwise coprime in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\).
  • Case II (\(p \mid xyz\)): Exactly one of \(x, y, z\) (say \(z\)) is divisible by \(p\), so every factor \(x + \zeta_p^j y\) is divisible by \(\lambda\).

5.1 Case I: When \(p \nmid xyz\)

In Case I, regularity implies that \(x + \zeta_p y = u \alpha^p\) for a unit \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) and an element \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\). Decomposing \(u = \zeta_p^r w\) with \(\bar{w} = w\) by Proposition 5 and reducing \(\alpha^p \equiv a \pmod{p}\) by Lemma 2 allows us to eliminate \(w\alpha^p\) against its complex conjugate \(\overline{w\alpha^p} \equiv w\alpha^p \pmod{p}\). The resulting congruence between \(x + \zeta_p y\) and \(\zeta_p^{2r}(x + \zeta_p^{-1} y)\) modulo \(p\) has at most four terms in the powers of \(\zeta_p\), which by Lemma 2 (2) forces \(x \equiv y \pmod{p}\) whenever \(p \ge 5\).

Theorem 7 (Kummer’s Theorem, Case I) Let \(p\) be a regular odd prime. There do not exist pairwise coprime integers \(x, y, z \in \mathbb{Z}\) with \(p \nmid xyz\) satisfying \(x^p + y^p + z^p = 0\).

Proof. First, if \(p = 3\), then for any integer \(m\) with \(3 \nmid m\) we have \(m \equiv \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 4 \pmod{9}\), so \(m^3 \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{9}\). Hence if \(3 \nmid xyz\), then \(x^3 + y^3 + z^3 \equiv \pm 1 \pm 1 \pm 1 \not\equiv 0 \pmod{9}\), so Case I holds for \(p = 3\). We may therefore assume that \(p \ge 5\).

Suppose toward a contradiction that \(x, y, z \in \mathbb{Z}\) are pairwise coprime with \(p \nmid xyz\) and \(x^p + y^p = (-z)^p\). As observed above, if \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta_p\) divided \(x + \zeta_p^j y\) for any \(j\), then \(\lambda\) would divide \(x + \zeta_p^i y \equiv x + \zeta_p^j y \pmod{\lambda}\) for every \(0 \le i \le p-1\), so \(\lambda^p\) (and hence \(p\)) would divide \(\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}(x + \zeta_p^i y) = -z^p\), contradicting \(p \nmid z\). Thus \(\lambda \nmid (x + \zeta_p^j y)\) for all \(j\).

By Proposition 2 (3), there exists an ideal \(\mathfrak{a} \subset \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) such that \((x + \zeta_p y)\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] = \mathfrak{a}^p\). Since \(p\) is regular, \(\mathfrak{a} = \alpha \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) is principal, so \(x + \zeta_p y = u \alpha^p\) for some unit \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) and \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\). By Proposition 5 (2) and Lemma 2 (1), we can write \(u = \zeta_p^r w\) with \(r \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(\bar{w} = w \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\), and \(\alpha^p \equiv a \pmod{p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]}\) for some \(a \in \mathbb{Z}\). Thus: \[x + \zeta_p y \equiv \zeta_p^r w a \pmod{p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]}\] Applying complex conjugation (which fixes \(x, y, a, p \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(w\), while sending \(\zeta_p \mapsto \zeta_p^{-1}\)) yields \(x + \zeta_p^{-1} y \equiv \zeta_p^{-r} w a \pmod{p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]}\). Multiplying this conjugate congruence by \(\zeta_p^{2r}\) and subtracting from the original congruence eliminates \(w a\) and gives: \[x + \zeta_p y - \zeta_p^{2r} x - \zeta_p^{2r-1} y \equiv 0 \pmod{p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]}\] We claim that the four exponents \(\{0, 1, 2r, 2r - 1\}\) cannot represent four distinct residue classes modulo \(p\). Indeed, since \(p \ge 5\), among any four distinct residue classes modulo \(p\), at most one can be \(p - 1 \pmod{p}\). Multiplying the congruence above by \(\zeta_p^{-k}\) where \(k \in \{0, 1, 2r, 2r - 1\}\) (if \(p - 1\) occurs among the four classes, choose \(k\) so that none of the shifted exponents is \(\equiv p - 1 \pmod{p}\), which is always possible since \(p \ge 5\)) produces a \(\mathbb{Z}\)-linear combination of four distinct basis elements from \(\{1, \zeta_p, \dots, \zeta_p^{p-2}\}\) with coefficients \(\pm x, \pm y\) that lies in \(p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\). By Lemma 2 (2), this would force \(p \mid x\) and \(p \mid y\), contradicting \(p \nmid xyz\).

Therefore, at least two of the residue classes \(\{0, 1\} \pmod{p}\) and \(\{2r, 2r - 1\} \pmod{p}\) must coincide:

  1. If \(2r \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\), then the congruence simplifies to \(\zeta_p y - \zeta_p^{p-1} y \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\). Multiplying by \(\zeta_p\) gives \((\zeta_p^2 - 1)y \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\), which by Lemma 2 (2) (since \(p \ge 5 > 3\)) forces \(p \mid y\), impossible.
  2. If \(2r - 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\), i.e., \(2r \equiv 2 \pmod{p}\), the congruence simplifies to \((1 - \zeta_p^2)x \equiv 0 \pmod{p}\), forcing \(p \mid x\) by Lemma 2 (2), impossible.
  3. Thus we must have \(2r \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\). Substituting \(2r \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\) into the congruence gives: \[(x - y) - (x - y)\zeta_p \equiv 0 \pmod{p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]}\] By Lemma 2 (2), this forces \(x \equiv y \pmod{p}\).

Since \(x^p + y^p + z^p = 0\) is completely symmetric in \(x, y, z\) and \(p \nmid xyz\), repeating the same argument with \((x, z)\) in place of \((x, y)\) yields \(x \equiv z \pmod{p}\). Consequently, \(0 = x^p + y^p + z^p \equiv x + y + z \equiv 3x \pmod{p}\). Since \(p \ge 5\), this forces \(p \mid x\), a contradiction.

5.2 Case II: Kummer’s Unit Lemma and Arithmetic Infinite Descent

When \(p \mid xyz\), say \(p \mid z\), the prime uniformizer \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta_p\) divides \(z\) and hence divides every factor \(x + \zeta_p^j y\). To carry out an infinite descent on the \(\lambda\)-adic valuation of \(z\), we work from the outset inside the ring \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) (just as Fermat’s proof for \(n = 4\) in Theorem 1 works with the generalized equation \(x^4 + y^4 = z^2\)) and consider the generalized Fermat equation: \[\alpha^p + \beta^p + u \lambda^{mp} \gamma^p = 0\] where \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) are pairwise coprime and not divisible by \(\lambda\), \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) is a unit, and \(m \ge 1\) is an integer (note that any primitive solution \(x^p + y^p + z^p = 0\) in \(\mathbb{Z}\) with \(p \mid z\) has \(\lambda^{p-1} = (\text{unit})\cdot p \mid z\), so \(v_\lambda(z) \ge p - 1 \ge 1\) and writing \(z = \text{unit} \cdot \lambda^m \gamma\) with \(\lambda \nmid \gamma\) produces a solution of this form).

At the culmination of the descent step, we encounter a unit \(\varepsilon \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) that is congruent to a rational integer modulo \(p\), and we must know that \(\varepsilon\) is a \(p\)-th power of a unit in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\). This is the content of Kummer’s Unit Lemma.

Lemma 3 (Kummer’s Unit Lemma for Regular Primes) Let \(p\) be a regular odd prime. If a unit \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) is congruent to a rational integer \(c \in \mathbb{Z}\) modulo \(p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\), then \(u\) is the \(p\)-th power of a unit in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\); that is, \(u \in (\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times)^p\).

Remark 7 (Elementary Case \(p = 3\) and the Class-Field Mechanism of Kummer’s Unit Lemma). For \(p = 3\) (where \(h_3 = 1\)), Lemma 3 is completely immediate: by Proposition 5 (2), any unit \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_3]^\times\) has the form \(u = \pm \zeta_3^r\) since the real subfield of \(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3)\) is \(\mathbb{Q}\) with unit group \(\{\pm 1\}\), and since \((-1)^3 = -1\) and \(\zeta_3 = (\zeta_3^2)^3\), every unit in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_3]^\times\) is already a cube.

For a general regular prime \(p \ge 5\), how does \(p \nmid h_p\) imply Lemma 3? Consider the Kummer extension \(L = K(u^{1/p})\) over \(K = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)\). For any prime ideal \(\mathfrak{q} \nmid p\) of \(\mathcal{O}_K\), the polynomial \(X^p - u\) has discriminant \(\pm p^p u^{p-1} \notin \mathfrak{q}\), so \(L/K\) is unramified at all primes \(\mathfrak{q} \nmid \mathfrak{p}\). At the unique prime \(\mathfrak{p} = (\lambda)\) above \(p\), the hypothesis \(u \equiv c \equiv c^p \pmod{p}\) implies \(u/c^p \equiv 1 \pmod{\lambda^{p-1}}\). If in fact \(u/c^p \equiv 1 \pmod{\lambda^p}\), then setting \(\theta = \frac{c^{-1}u^{1/p} - 1}{\lambda}\) satisfies a monic integral equation over \(\mathcal{O}_K\) whose discriminant is coprime to \(\mathfrak{p}\), proving that \(L/K\) is everywhere unramified. Because \(L/K\) is an unramified abelian extension of degree dividing \(p\), class field theory (specifically, the isomorphism \(\operatorname{Gal}(H_K/K) \cong \operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_K)\) for the Hilbert class field \(H_K\) of \(K\)) and the regularity hypothesis \(p \nmid |\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_K)|\) force \(L = K\), so \(u \in (K^\times)^p \cap \mathcal{O}_K^\times = (\mathcal{O}_K^\times)^p\). Ruling out the boundary congruence \(u/c^p \equiv 1 + k\lambda^{p-1} \pmod{\lambda^p}\) with \(p \nmid k\) uses the \(p\)-adic logarithm and the analytic class number formula linking \(p \nmid h_p^-\) to the non-vanishing of the \(p\)-adic regulator modulo \(p\) (Washington 1997, Theorem 5.36).

Before executing the Case II descent, we isolate a valuation lemma showing that in \(\alpha^p + \beta^p = u \lambda^{mp} \gamma^p\) with \(\lambda \nmid \alpha\beta\gamma\), the exponent \(m\) must be at least \(2\), and the exact \(\lambda\)-adic valuation of every factor \(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta\) is completely determined.

Lemma 4 (Exact \(\lambda\)-adic Valuations in Case II) Let \(p\) be an odd prime, \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta_p\), and suppose \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) are pairwise coprime elements not divisible by \(\lambda\) satisfying: \[\alpha^p + \beta^p = u \lambda^{mp} \gamma^p\] for some unit \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) and integer \(m \ge 1\). Then:

  1. We have \(m \ge 2\).
  2. After replacing \(\beta\) by \(\zeta_p^{j_0}\beta\) for a suitable \(j_0 \in \{0, \dots, p-1\}\), we have \(v_\lambda(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta) = 1\) for every \(1 \le j \le p - 1\), and \(v_\lambda(\alpha + \beta) = mp - (p - 1) \ge p + 1\).
  3. The \(p\) ideals \(\mathfrak{b}_0 := \left(\frac{\alpha + \beta}{\lambda^{mp - (p-1)}}\right)\) and \(\mathfrak{b}_j := \left(\frac{\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta}{1 - \zeta_p^j}\right)\) (\(1 \le j \le p - 1\)) in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) are pairwise coprime and coprime to \(\lambda\), and each is the \(p\)-th power of an ideal \(\mathfrak{a}_j \subset \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\).

Proof. Because \(\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta) = u \lambda^{mp} \gamma^p\) and \(m \ge 1\), the prime \(\lambda\) divides \(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta\) for at least one \(j\). Since \(\zeta_p^j \equiv 1 \pmod{\lambda}\), we have \(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta \equiv \alpha + \beta \pmod{\lambda}\) for all \(j\), so \(\lambda\) divides \(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta\) for every \(0 \le j \le p - 1\).

Consider the quotients \(\frac{\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta}{\lambda} \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) for \(0 \le j \le p - 1\). Since \(\frac{1 - \zeta_p^j}{\lambda} = 1 + \zeta_p + \dots + \zeta_p^{j-1} \equiv j \pmod{\lambda}\), we have: \[\frac{\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta}{\lambda} = \frac{\alpha + \beta}{\lambda} - \frac{1 - \zeta_p^j}{\lambda}\beta \equiv \frac{\alpha + \beta}{\lambda} - j\beta \pmod{\lambda}\] Because \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]/(\lambda) \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\) is a field of \(p\) elements and \(\beta \not\equiv 0 \pmod{\lambda}\), as \(j\) ranges over \(0, 1, \dots, p - 1\), the residue classes \(\frac{\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta}{\lambda} \pmod{\lambda}\) run through all \(p\) distinct elements of \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]/(\lambda)\). In particular, there is a unique index \(j_0 \in \{0, \dots, p - 1\}\) such that \(\frac{\alpha + \zeta_p^{j_0}\beta}{\lambda} \equiv 0 \pmod{\lambda}\), meaning \(v_\lambda(\alpha + \zeta_p^{j_0}\beta) \ge 2\), whereas for all \(j \neq j_0\) we have \(v_\lambda(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta) = 1\).

Replacing \(\beta\) by \(\zeta_p^{j_0}\beta\) (which preserves \(\lambda \nmid \beta\), \((\alpha, \beta) = 1\), and \(\beta^p\)), we have \(j_0 = 0\), so \(v_\lambda(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta) = 1\) for all \(1 \le j \le p - 1\) and \(v_\lambda(\alpha + \beta) \ge 2\). Summing valuations over \(0 \le j \le p - 1\) gives: \[mp = \sum_{j=0}^{p-1} v_\lambda(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta) = v_\lambda(\alpha + \beta) + (p - 1) \ge 2 + (p - 1) = p + 1 > p\] Since \(m\) is an integer, this forces \(m \ge 2\) and \(v_\lambda(\alpha + \beta) = mp - (p - 1) \ge p + 1\), proving (1) and (2).

For (3), by (2) we have \(v_\lambda(\mathfrak{b}_j) = 0\) for all \(0 \le j \le p-1\). If a prime ideal \(\mathfrak{q} \neq (\lambda)\) divided two distinct \(\mathfrak{b}_i\) and \(\mathfrak{b}_j\), then \(\mathfrak{q}\) would divide \((\alpha + \zeta_p^i \beta)\) and \((\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta)\), hence by the proof of Proposition 2 (2) would divide \(\lambda(\alpha, \beta)\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p] = (\lambda)\), impossible. Since \(\prod_{j=1}^{p-1}(1 - \zeta_p^j) = p = (\text{unit})\cdot \lambda^{p-1}\), the product of the pairwise coprime ideals \(\mathfrak{b}_0, \dots, \mathfrak{b}_{p-1}\) is: \[\prod_{j=0}^{p-1} \mathfrak{b}_j = \left(\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}(\alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta)}{\lambda^{mp - (p-1)} \prod_{j=1}^{p-1}(1 - \zeta_p^j)}\right) = \left(\frac{u \lambda^{mp} \gamma^p}{\lambda^{mp}}\right) = (\gamma)^p\] Thus each \(\mathfrak{b}_j = \mathfrak{a}_j^p\) is the \(p\)-th power of an ideal \(\mathfrak{a}_j \subset \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\).

We now complete the proof of Kummer’s theorem by combining Lemma 4, the three-term linear elimination from Theorem 2, and Lemma 3.

Theorem 8 (Kummer’s Theorem, Case II (and Full FLT for Regular Primes)) Let \(p\) be a regular odd prime. For any integer \(m \ge 1\), the generalized Fermat equation \(\alpha^p + \beta^p = u \lambda^{mp} \gamma^p\) has no solutions with \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) pairwise coprime and coprime to \(\lambda = 1 - \zeta_p\), and \(u \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\). In particular, combining this with Theorem 7, the equation \(x^p + y^p = z^p\) has no primitive solutions in \(\mathbb{Z}\).

Proof. Suppose toward a contradiction that such solutions \((\alpha, \beta, \gamma, u, m)\) exist, and choose one minimizing the positive integer \(m \ge 1\). By Lemma 4 (1)–(3), we have \(m \ge 2\), and after multiplying \(\beta\) by a power of \(\zeta_p\), each ideal \(\mathfrak{b}_j\) (\(0 \le j \le p-1\)) is the \(p\)-th power \(\mathfrak{a}_j^p\) of an ideal in \(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\). Because \(p\) is regular (\(p \nmid h_p\)), each \(\mathfrak{a}_j = (\tau_j)\) is principal, where \(\tau_0, \tau_1, \dots, \tau_{p-1} \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) are pairwise coprime and not divisible by \(\lambda\). Therefore, there exist units \(u_0, u_1, \dots, u_{p-1} \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) such that: \[\alpha + \beta = u_0 \lambda^{mp - (p-1)} \tau_0^p, \qquad \alpha + \zeta_p^j \beta = u_j (1 - \zeta_p^j) \tau_j^p \quad (1 \le j \le p - 1)\] Just as in the polynomial infinite descent (Theorem 2), we eliminate \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) from the three equations for \(j = 0, 1, 2\) using the linear identity \((1 - \zeta_p)(\alpha + \zeta_p^2 \beta) - (1 - \zeta_p^2)(\alpha + \zeta_p \beta) + (\zeta_p - \zeta_p^2)(\alpha + \beta) = 0\). Substituting the expressions above and dividing through by \((1 - \zeta_p)(1 - \zeta_p^2) = \lambda^2 (1 + \zeta_p)\) (noting that \(1 + \zeta_p = \frac{1 - \zeta_p^2}{1 - \zeta_p} \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) and \(\zeta_p - \zeta_p^2 = \zeta_p \lambda\)) yields: \[u_2 \tau_2^p - u_1 \tau_1^p + \frac{\zeta_p u_0}{1 + \zeta_p} \lambda^{mp - p} \tau_0^p = 0\] Dividing by \(-u_1\) and setting the units \(\varepsilon := u_2 / u_1 \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) and \(\varepsilon' := \frac{\zeta_p u_0}{(1 + \zeta_p)u_1} \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\), we obtain: \[\tau_1^p - \varepsilon \tau_2^p = \varepsilon' \lambda^{(m-1)p} \tau_0^p\] Because \(m \ge 2\), we have \((m - 1)p \ge p\), so the right-hand side is divisible by \(\lambda^p\) (and hence by \(p\)). Reducing modulo \(\lambda^p\) gives \(\tau_1^p \equiv \varepsilon \tau_2^p \pmod{\lambda^p}\). By Lemma 2 (1), there exist rational integers \(t_1, t_2 \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(\tau_1^p \equiv t_1 \pmod{\lambda^p}\) and \(\tau_2^p \equiv t_2 \pmod{\lambda^p}\). Since \(\lambda \nmid \tau_2\), we have \(p \nmid t_2\), so choosing \(t_2^* \in \mathbb{Z}\) with \(t_2 t_2^* \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\) shows that \(\varepsilon \equiv t_1 t_2^* \pmod{p\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]}\).

By Kummer’s Unit Lemma (Lemma 3), since \(p\) is regular and \(\varepsilon \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) is congruent to a rational integer modulo \(p\), there exists a unit \(\eta \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]^\times\) such that \(\varepsilon = \eta^p\). Setting \(\tilde{\alpha} = \tau_1\), \(\tilde{\beta} = -\eta \tau_2\), and \(\tilde{\gamma} = \tau_0\), we find that \(\tilde{\alpha}, \tilde{\beta}, \tilde{\gamma} \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]\) are pairwise coprime and coprime to \(\lambda\), and satisfy: \[\tilde{\alpha}^p + \tilde{\beta}^p = \varepsilon' \lambda^{(m-1)p} \tilde{\gamma}^p\] with \(m - 1 \ge 1\) strictly smaller than \(m\). This contradicts the minimality of \(m\) and completes the infinite descent.
